## 1. Introduction — Why Offers Fail

When a small business struggles to sell, the problem is often not the marketing. It's the offer. Typical symptoms:

| Symptom | Usual cause in the offer |
|---|---|
| "Let me think about it" — and they never return | The outcome is unclear, or the price isn't connected to a result |
| Every customer wants a custom quote | No standard package; scope not defined |
| Projects keep growing without extra payment | No written boundaries or change process |
| Customers compare you only on price | Offer looks identical to alternatives |
| Disputes and refund requests | Terms, exclusions or timelines weren't written down |
| You dread delivering it | The offer doesn't match your capacity or strengths |

The **Offer Lab** fixes these in seven steps. Tool 4.2 in the Business Strategy Workbook gave you the eight-part Offer Stack (W-STR-12). The Offer Lab takes that first draft and makes it **sales-ready**: a clear promise, a defined delivery path, written scope, sensible terms, a name and a one-page document you can send to customers.

**Who it's for:** service businesses, freelancers, consultants, coaches, and product sellers creating bundles or premium packages.
**Time:** 2–3 hours for one offer (can be split over several days).

## 4. Step 2 — Before → After Map and Promise Boundary

**Purpose:** describe the change your customer gets, and separate what you can promise from what you can't.

### The Before → After Map

Describe the customer's situation before and after across four dimensions:

| Dimension | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| **Have** — what they possess or have in place | | |
| **Do** — what their days or routines look like | | |
| **Feel** — their emotional state about this problem | | |
| **Avoid** — the risks or costs they no longer face | | |

### The Promise Boundary

Sort every outcome into three columns:

| I CONTROL — can promise | I INFLUENCE — can describe, not guarantee | I DON'T CONTROL — must not promise |
|---|---|---|
| What I deliver, by when, to what standard | Likely results if the customer participates | Results that depend on the market, the customer's actions, third parties or chance |
| *e.g. 8 posts/week, monthly report by the 5th* | *e.g. more consistent enquiries* | *e.g. a specific number of sales or patients* |

**Rule:** your offer's promises come only from the first column. The second column is described honestly ("designed to help…", "customers who complete all steps typically…" — only if you have real data). The third column never appears as a promise.

## 5. Step 3 — Delivery Path

**Purpose:** design how the customer moves through the offer, so it can be delivered the same way every time — by you or your team.

Every offer has four stages:

| Stage | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **START** | How does the customer begin? | Payment, welcome message, intake form, kick-off call, booking a slot |
| **DELIVER** | What happens, in what order? | Visits, sessions, files, milestones, check-ins |
| **FINISH** | How does it end clearly? | Final report, handover, completion message, feedback request |
| **AFTER** | What happens next? | Review request, maintenance offer, renewal, referral invitation |

Most offers lose customers' trust at START (slow, confusing onboarding) or at FINISH (the work fades out with no clear end). Design both deliberately.

## 6. Step 4 — Scope & Boundaries

**Purpose:** prevent scope creep, disputes and unpaid extra work by writing down exactly what is and isn't included.

Four parts of a scope:

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| **Included** | Every deliverable, with quantity and standard |
| **Not included** | Things customers commonly assume are included but aren't |
| **Customer responsibilities** | What the customer must provide or do, and by when (access, information, approvals, payments) |
| **Change process** | What happens when the customer asks for more: how it's quoted, approved and scheduled |

## 7. Step 5 — Packaging, Extras and Terms

**Purpose:** decide how the offer is packaged, which extras add value, and which terms protect both sides.

### Packaging

Decide whether this offer is sold as a single package or in tiers. If tiers, run **P-STR-023 (Offer Tier Designer)** from the workbook. Rules of thumb:

- The **Core** tier is the one you most want to sell. Design it first.
- **Entry** removes elements; it shouldn't be the Core with a discount.
- **Premium** adds speed, access, depth or done-for-you elements — not just "more".

### Extras (bonuses) — the three tests

Only include an extra if it passes all three:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Useful** | Does it help the customer get the outcome faster, easier or more completely? |
| **Low-cost to deliver** | Can you provide it without significant extra time or money per customer? |
| **Honest value** | Can you describe its value without inflated "worth ₹X" claims you can't substantiate? |

Good extras are often templates, checklists, priority scheduling, a follow-up check-in, or a resource you have already made.

### Terms — the fair-terms checklist

| Term | Decide | ☐ |
|---|---|---|
| Payment | Amount, timing (advance / milestone / monthly), method | ☐ |
| Start | What triggers the start (payment received, form completed, slot booked) | ☐ |
| Timeline | When each part is delivered | ☐ |
| Rescheduling | Notice required; what happens if either side reschedules | ☐ |
| Cancellation | How either side can cancel; notice period; what is charged | ☐ |
| Refunds | When refunds apply and how they're calculated — clear and fair | ☐ |
| Minimum term | For subscriptions/retainers — and what happens after | ☐ |
| Exclusions | Linked to your scope (Step 4) | ☐ |
| Customer responsibilities | Linked to your scope (Step 4) | ☐ |

> **Important.** Refund, cancellation and consumer-protection requirements vary by country, product type and sales channel (e.g. marketplaces have their own policies). Treat these as business decisions to be checked: **[VERIFY CURRENT INFORMATION]** and consult a qualified professional before publishing terms.

## 8. Step 6 — Name the Offer

**Purpose:** give the offer a name that is clear first and memorable second.

Good offer names usually follow one of four patterns:

| Pattern | Formula | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|---|
| **Descriptive** | [Outcome/Thing] + [Format] | "Monthly Home Care Plan" |
| **Brand + descriptive** | [Brand] + [Thing] | "BrightNest Care Plan" |
| **Outcome + time** | [Outcome] in [Timeframe] | "12-Week Floor Reset" |
| **Method** | The [Name] Method/System | "The Enquiry Engine" (only if there is a real, defined method) |

**Naming rules:**
1. A stranger should guess roughly what it is from the name alone — or the name must always appear with a descriptor.
2. Easy to say on a phone call and type into WhatsApp.
3. No exaggerated words ("ultimate", "guaranteed", "secret") or names that imply results you can't promise.
4. Check that the name isn't already used by a well-known business in your field. **[VERIFY CURRENT INFORMATION]** — for trademark questions, consult a qualified professional.

### Example 1 — Business F: Northbridge Ops Advisory (consultant)

| Element | Decision |
|---|---|
| **Name** | **12-Week Floor Reset** — *hands-on operations improvement for small manufacturers* |
| For / not for | For owner-led manufacturers with 20–150 staff facing delivery delays or rework. Not for businesses wanting a report only, or unwilling to involve floor supervisors. |
| Problem | "Orders go out late and we don't know exactly why." |
| Before → After | **Have:** no clear view of bottlenecks → a simple daily production board and 3 documented routines. **Do:** firefighting daily → a 15-minute daily review meeting. **Feel:** anxious about key accounts → in control of what's late and why. **Avoid:** repeated penalties and rework from avoidable errors. |

| Delivery path | START: diagnostic visit + data request + kick-off with owner · DELIVER: weeks 1–4 map and measure; weeks 5–10 implement routines with supervisors; weekly owner review · FINISH: week 12 handover report and routine checklists · AFTER: 30-day follow-up visit; optional monthly review retainer |

## 12. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Promising results you don't control | Promise deliverables and process; describe likely outcomes honestly |
| Inflated "bonus worth ₹X" claims | Describe what the extra does, not an invented value |
| Scope written after the dispute | Write scope before the first sale; include it in the one-pager |
| A clever name nobody understands | Clarity first; always pair with a descriptor line |
| Offer designed beyond capacity | Do the capacity check in Step 3 |
| Fake urgency ("only 2 spots left!") when untrue | State real limits factually, or none at all |
